How to read a spectrogram

Time runs left to right and frequency runs bottom to top; the colour is how loud each frequency is at that moment, from black (silent) through purple and orange to pale yellow (loud). A sung or played note is a stack of horizontal lines, its fundamental at the bottom and the harmonics above at two, three and four times its frequency, which is why a voice looks like a set of parallel curves. Drums are vertical smears: a kick at the bottom, a snare across the middle, hi-hats as thin streaks at the top. Speech alternates between those voiced stacks and the noisy blurs of "s", "sh" and "f".

The log scale gives each octave the same height, the way you hear pitch, so a melody is easy to follow. The linear scale gives each hertz the same height, which spreads out the top of the spectrum: that is the scale for checking where a file's high end stops, or for spotting a steady whine at a fixed frequency.

What people use it for

Checking a file's quality. An MP3 or AAC leaves a hard ceiling where its encoder cut the top off, around 16 kHz at 128 kbps, and a "lossless" file made from one keeps that ceiling. Set Top to all of it and look for a flat edge. The fake lossless detector measures it and names the likely bitrate.

Finding noise. Mains hum is a ladder of thin lines at 50 or 60 Hz and its multiples, for which there is the hum remover. A vinyl click is a single bright vertical line, which the click remover repairs. A fan or air conditioning is a soft band that never changes, and a dropout is a vertical gap.

Birds, bats and the rest of nature. Birdsong is easier to tell apart by its shape than by ear, which is why apps like Merlin and BirdNET draw a spectrogram as they listen. A recording at 96 or 192 kHz shows bat calls and other ultrasound far above anything audible; set the range to 120 dB to see faint ones.

Hidden images. Artists have drawn pictures into the spectrum: Aphex Twin hid a face in a track from the Windowlicker single, and Mick Gordon hid pentagrams in the Doom (2016) soundtrack. They only appear in a spectrogram.

Detail: sharp in time or sharp in pitch

A spectrogram cannot be sharp in both at once. Each column comes from a short window of the sound: a long window tells frequencies apart finely but blurs when they happen, and a short one does the opposite. At 48 kHz, 4096 points, the default, is an 85 ms window with 11.7 Hz between frequency bins, a good balance for music. 8192 separates the low notes of a bass line or the lines of a hum. 1024, a 21 ms window, pins down drum hits and consonants. This page also analyses only what is on screen, one window per pixel column. Zooming in shows real detail rather than an enlarged picture, and when zoomed far out, each column takes the loudest of up to eight windows across its stretch, so a click between two columns is not lost.

The check behind this page requires a 1 kHz tone to land on the 1 kHz row on both scales, two tones to show as two separate peaks with nothing between them, a sweep from 100 Hz to 10 kHz to stay on its row (within one frequency bin) as it rises, a 2 ms click in a minute-long file to light its column when zoomed all the way out, and the saved PNG to be the full 1600 x 900. Spek, Sonic Visualiser and Audacity's spectrogram view are good desktop alternatives; iZotope RX and Adobe Audition let you edit inside the spectrogram, which this page does not.

FAQ

How do I see the spectrogram of an audio file?

Drop the file above. The spectrogram is drawn straight away; drag across it to zoom in, move over it to read the time, frequency and level, and press Save PNG to keep the picture.

Log or linear: which scale should I use?

Log for music and voices, because it matches how we hear pitch. Linear for checking the top of the spectrum, such as where an MP3 cut its high end, or a fixed whine.

What FFT size should I choose?

4096 suits most music. Choose 8192 to separate low notes or hum lines, and 1024 to see exactly when drum hits and consonants happen.

Can I see frequencies above 20 kHz?

Yes, if the file has them. A 96 kHz recording shows up to 48 kHz, which reveals bat calls, ultrasonic noise and whether a hi-res file was really upsampled from CD quality.

Can I save the spectrogram as an image?

Yes. Save PNG renders the current view at 1600 x 900 with frequency and time axes, a colour scale and the file name.

Is my file uploaded?

No. The file is decoded and analysed in your browser tab, so it works offline once the page has loaded.
